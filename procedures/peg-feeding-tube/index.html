Feeding tube (PEG)

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

For people who cannot swallow safely, a soft feeding tube is placed through the skin into the stomach with the help of the scope.

How it works

A PEG (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy) is a soft feeding tube placed through the skin of the upper belly directly into the stomach, with the help of an endoscope. It provides food, water and medicines when a person cannot swallow safely or enough by mouth for several weeks or longer.

It is more comfortable than a tube through the nose for long-term use, is hidden under clothing, and lowers the chance of food going into the lungs in people with swallowing problems. A PEG can be removed if swallowing recovers.

What it is used for

  • After a stroke that affects swallowing
  • Head, neck or food pipe cancers
  • Long-term neurological conditions
  • Anyone who cannot swallow safely for weeks or more

Before the procedure

  • Nothing to eat or drink through the mouth or any existing nose tube for 6 to 8 hours before.
  • Blood tests, including clotting, are checked. Blood thinners may need to be paused as planned by the doctor.
  • An antibiotic is usually given just before the procedure to prevent infection.
  • The decision is discussed with the patient and family, including how feeding will be managed at home.

On the day

The procedure is done under sedation, with a local anaesthetic at the skin. The endoscope shows the best spot on the stomach, a small cut is made in the skin, and the tube is guided into place and held by a soft disc inside the stomach and a small plate outside. It takes about 20 to 30 minutes.

After the procedure

  • Feeds are usually started within a day, beginning with water and then slowly increasing.
  • Keep the person sitting up at least halfway during feeds and for about an hour after.
  • Flush the tube with clean water before and after each feed and medicine to prevent blockage.
  • Clean the skin around the tube daily and keep it dry. The nurse will show you how to clean and turn the tube once the site has healed.
  • A dietitian advises on the type and amount of feed.

Risks

Mild pain at the site for a few days, minor infection, leakage around the tube and blockage are the most common problems and are usually easy to treat. Serious complications such as bleeding or injury to nearby organs are uncommon.

Get medical help straight away if you have

  • Pain during feeds, or feed leaking around the tube with redness or pus
  • Fever, vomiting or a swollen belly
  • The tube coming out: cover the site and get help within a few hours, as the opening can close quickly

Other tests and options

A tube through the nose (nasogastric tube) is used for short periods of a few weeks. A feeding tube can also be placed by a radiologist or surgeon when endoscopy is not possible.

Common questions

Can the patient still eat by mouth?

It depends on how safe swallowing is. Some people take small amounts by mouth along with tube feeds.

How long does the tube last?

Many months. It can be changed when needed, and removed if swallowing recovers.

Is getting a PEG painful?

It is done under sedation and local anaesthetic. Some soreness at the site for a few days is common and settles with simple painkillers.

Can medicines be given through the tube?

Yes, most can, as liquids or crushed and mixed with water, but some tablets must not be crushed. Check each medicine with your doctor or pharmacist.

What should I do if the tube comes out?

Cover the site with a clean dressing and contact the doctor or hospital straight away. The opening starts closing within hours, so prompt replacement is important.
