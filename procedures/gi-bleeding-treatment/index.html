Bleeding control

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Updated 17 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Bleeding from an ulcer or a damaged blood vessel is stopped through the scope with tiny clips, injections or heat, usually without surgery.

How it works

Bleeding from the digestive tract shows up as vomiting blood or coffee-ground material, black and sticky stools, or red blood from the back passage. It can cause weakness, dizziness or fainting, and needs emergency care.

After the patient is stabilised with fluids and, if needed, blood transfusion, an endoscopy is done, usually within 24 hours. The bleeding point is found and treated through the scope with tiny clips, an injection, heat, bands for varices, glue for stomach varices, or a special powder. Common causes are stomach or duodenal ulcers (often from painkillers or H. pylori), varices in liver disease and abnormal blood vessels.

What it is used for

  • Bleeding stomach or duodenal ulcers
  • Bleeding from varices in liver disease
  • Bleeding from abnormal blood vessels
  • Bleeding after polyp removal

Before the procedure

  • This is usually an emergency procedure. Go to the nearest emergency department or call 112 if you vomit blood or pass black stools.
  • In hospital, a drip, blood tests and blood transfusion are given as needed, along with medicines through the vein.
  • Nothing is eaten until the endoscopy is done.
  • Bring a list of all your medicines, especially painkillers and blood thinners. Do not stop or start any medicine without the treating team's advice.

On the day

The endoscopy is done under sedation, sometimes with an anaesthetist present if the bleeding is heavy. Most bleeding points can be treated in 20 to 45 minutes. Upper bleeding is treated through an upper endoscopy; bleeding from the colon is treated through a colonoscopy after preparation.

After the procedure

  • You stay in hospital, usually for 1 to 3 days, to make sure the bleeding does not return.
  • Food is restarted gradually once bleeding has stopped.
  • For ulcers, acid-reducing medicines are given and H. pylori is tested and treated.
  • Painkillers that cause ulcers are stopped where possible, and blood thinners are restarted when safe.
  • Iron or other treatment for anaemia is given, and a repeat endoscopy is sometimes planned to check healing.

Risks

Endoscopy stops most bleeding. Sometimes bleeding returns, and a repeat endoscopy, a radiology procedure (embolisation) or surgery may be needed. Complications of the endoscopy itself, such as a tear, are uncommon.

Get medical help straight away if you have

  • Vomiting blood again or passing more black or red stools
  • Dizziness, fainting, fast heartbeat or breathlessness
  • Severe belly pain

Other tests and options

If endoscopy cannot stop the bleeding, an interventional radiologist can block the bleeding vessel from inside (embolisation), and surgery is the last option. For varices, medicines and TIPS may be used along with endoscopy.

Common questions

What should I do if I vomit blood or pass black stools?

Go to the nearest emergency department straight away, or call 112. Do not wait to see if it settles.

Can the bleeding come back?

Sometimes. Treating the cause, such as clearing H. pylori or stopping painkillers, greatly lowers the risk.

Why do painkillers cause bleeding ulcers?

Common painkillers such as NSAIDs reduce the stomach's natural protection, especially in older people and with long use. Ask your doctor before taking them regularly.

Will I need a blood transfusion?

Only if the blood count is low or bleeding is heavy. Many people do not need one.

How soon is the endoscopy done?

Usually within 24 hours of arriving in hospital, and sooner if the bleeding is heavy or there are signs of varices.
