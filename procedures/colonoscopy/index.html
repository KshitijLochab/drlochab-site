Colonoscopy

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Updated 17 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

A camera passed through the back passage to examine the large bowel. Polyps found during the test are removed at the same time.

How it works

A colonoscopy examines the whole large bowel (colon) and the end of the small intestine with a thin, flexible camera passed through the back passage. It is done under sedation, so most people feel little or nothing.

It is the most accurate test for blood in the stool, a lasting change in bowel habit, unexplained anaemia and inflammatory bowel disease. Its biggest advantage is that polyps, which can slowly turn into cancer, can be found and removed during the same test.

What it is used for

  • Blood in the stool
  • Change in bowel habit lasting weeks
  • Unexplained anaemia or weight loss
  • Checking and following up IBD
  • Screening for polyps and colon cancer

Before the procedure

  • Eat a low-fibre diet for 1 to 2 days before: avoid seeds, salads, whole grains, dal with skins and fruits with peel.
  • The day before, switch to clear liquids such as water, clear soup, coconut water and light-coloured juices without pulp.
  • Take the bowel cleansing drink exactly as instructed, usually split between the evening before and the morning of the test. Your stools should become clear, yellow liquid.
  • Stop iron tablets about a week before, as they leave dark residue in the bowel.
  • Tell the doctor about blood thinners, diabetes medicines and insulin, which may need changes.
  • Come with an adult who can take you home after sedation.

On the day

You lie on your left side and are given sedation through a vein. The scope is passed gently around the colon while air or carbon dioxide opens it up for a clear view. Polyps are removed and biopsies taken as needed, which is painless. The test usually takes 20 to 40 minutes.

After the procedure

  • You rest for about an hour. Bloating and passing wind for a few hours are normal.
  • Eat normally afterwards unless you are given special advice after polyp removal.
  • Do not drive or make important decisions for the rest of the day.
  • A streak of blood after polyp removal can occur. Biopsy and polyp reports take about a week.

Risks

Colonoscopy is safe for most people. Bloating and cramps for a few hours are common. Bleeding after polyp removal happens in a small number of people, and a tear in the bowel wall is rare, more so after removing large polyps. Reactions to sedation are uncommon.

Get medical help straight away if you have

  • Severe or worsening belly pain, or a hard, swollen belly
  • More than a few spoonfuls of blood from the back passage
  • Fever, dizziness or fainting

Other tests and options

Stool tests for hidden blood and CT colonography can help in some situations, but neither can take biopsies or remove polyps. If they are abnormal, a colonoscopy is still needed.

Common questions

Is the bowel preparation difficult?

The cleansing drink is the hardest part, but it is important: a clean bowel lets small polyps be seen. Follow the timing exactly.

Will I feel pain?

Usually not. It is done under sedation, and you may feel some pressure or wind afterwards.

How long does a colonoscopy take?

Usually 20 to 40 minutes, plus time for preparation and recovery. Plan for about 2 to 3 hours at the hospital.

When should I have a screening colonoscopy?

Many guidelines suggest starting around age 45 to 50 for people at average risk. If a parent, brother or sister had colon cancer or large polyps, screening usually starts earlier. Discuss your own risk with your doctor.

What if my bowel preparation is not clear?

Small polyps can be missed, and the test may need to be repeated. Following the diet and drink timing carefully makes a big difference.
