Opening narrowings

Dilatation & stenting

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Updated 17 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Narrowed sections of the food pipe, bile duct or bowel are widened with a balloon, or kept open with a stent placed through the scope.

How it works

A narrowing (stricture) in the food pipe, bile duct or bowel can cause food to stick, vomiting, jaundice or blockage. Narrowings can follow acid damage, swallowing chemicals, surgery or radiation, or be caused by a growth.

During an endoscopy, the narrowing can be gently stretched with a balloon or tapered dilators. In achalasia, a condition where the lower food pipe does not relax, a larger balloon is used to stretch the muscle. When a growth is blocking the food pipe, bile duct, duodenum or colon, a stent (a flexible mesh tube) can be placed to keep it open and relieve symptoms.

What it is used for

  • Narrowings of the food pipe from acid, chemicals, surgery or radiation
  • Achalasia (balloon dilatation)
  • Blocked bile ducts
  • Growths blocking the food pipe or bowel, to relieve symptoms

Before the procedure

  • Nothing to eat for 6 to 8 hours before. For achalasia, liquids only for a day or two before may be advised, so that the food pipe is empty.
  • Tell the doctor about blood thinners and diabetes medicines.
  • Bring previous endoscopy, barium and scan reports.
  • Come with an adult who can take you home.

On the day

The procedure is done under sedation, sometimes with X-ray guidance to position a balloon or stent accurately. It usually takes 20 to 45 minutes. Many narrowings need more than one session, a few weeks apart, to open fully.

After the procedure

  • Start with sips of water after a few hours, then liquids and soft food, before slowly returning to normal food.
  • Mild chest discomfort for a day is common after dilatation of the food pipe.
  • With a food pipe stent: eat sitting upright, take small bites, chew very well, and take sips of water during meals. Avoid large pieces of meat and doughy bread.
  • Plan follow-up as advised, as some narrowings need repeat sessions.

Risks

Chest discomfort is common for a day. A tear in the wall is uncommon but is the most important risk, especially with achalasia dilatation, and usually shows as severe pain. Stents can sometimes move, block with food or tissue, or cause pain, and may need adjustment or replacement.

Get medical help straight away if you have

  • Severe chest, neck or belly pain after the procedure
  • Fever, breathlessness or vomiting blood
  • Food sticking again or being unable to swallow saliva

Other tests and options

Surgery is an option for some narrowings and for achalasia. Medicines and repeated dilatation are used depending on the cause. The best option depends on the reason for the narrowing, your overall health and your preferences.

Common questions

How many dilatations will I need?

Some narrowings need one session; others need several, a few weeks apart.

Is a stent permanent?

It depends on the reason. Stents for growths often stay; others are removed after some weeks.

Will I be able to eat normally after dilatation?

Most people swallow much better after one or more sessions. If the narrowing returns, the dilatation can be repeated.

What is achalasia?

A condition in which the muscle at the lower end of the food pipe does not relax, so food and liquids collect above it. It causes difficulty swallowing, regurgitation and weight loss, and is treated by stretching or cutting the tight muscle.

How long does a stent last?

Stents placed for growths are usually left in place for symptom relief. Stents for non-cancer narrowings are often removed after some weeks.
