Gut health after 60: common problems, diet and when to get checked

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Updated 12 October 2026

With age the gut slows down, more medicines are taken, and some serious conditions, such as bowel cancer, become more common. Many problems in older people are still simple to treat, but new symptoms after 60 deserve a proper check instead of being put down to age.

In short

Constipation, acidity, poor appetite and swallowing trouble are common after 60, and are often linked to medicines, drinking too little water and less activity. Weight loss, anaemia, blood in the stool, black stools or any new symptom should always be checked. Review all your medicines with your doctor at least once a year.

What changes with age

The gut

Slower digestion

  • Food moves more slowly, so constipation and bloating are common
  • Thirst is felt less, so people drink less water
  • Teeth or denture problems make chewing harder

Medicines

More tablets, more side effects

  • Painkillers can cause ulcers and bleeding
  • Iron, calcium and some blood pressure and pain medicines cause constipation
  • Blood thinners raise the risk of bleeding from the gut

Risk

Some diseases become more common

  • Colon polyps and bowel cancer
  • Gallstones and bile duct stones
  • Ulcers and bleeding, especially with painkillers

Common problems and what helps

Constipation

The most common gut complaint after 60, made worse by drinking too little, walking less and some medicines.

What helps

  • 6 to 8 glasses of water a day, unless your doctor has limited fluids for heart or kidney disease
  • Fibre from fruit, vegetables, dal and whole grains, or isabgol with plenty of water
  • A walk every day, even a short one
  • A regular toilet time after breakfast, with the feet resting on a low stool
  • Ask your doctor whether any of your medicines could be the cause
  • Avoid daily use of strong laxatives without advice

New constipation, a change in bowel habit lasting more than 3 weeks, blood in the stool or weight loss needs a check, usually with a colonoscopy, to rule out a growth.

Acidity, ulcers and painkillers

Painkillers taken for joint pain, such as ibuprofen and diclofenac, are a common cause of ulcers and bleeding in older people, often without much pain beforehand.

What helps

  • Take painkillers only when needed, and ask your doctor about safer options for joint pain
  • If you take aspirin or a blood thinner, do not stop it on your own; ask whether you need stomach protection
  • Smaller meals, and dinner at least 2 to 3 hours before bed
  • Raise the head end of the bed if acidity is worse at night

New acidity after 60, difficulty swallowing, weight loss, anaemia, vomiting or black stools need an endoscopy.

Swallowing difficulty

Food sticking in the throat or chest, or coughing and choking while eating, is not a normal part of ageing.

What helps

  • Sit upright for meals and for 30 minutes afterwards
  • Take small bites and eat slowly
  • Choose soft, moist foods
  • Get teeth and dentures checked

Food sticking, or swallowing that is getting harder, needs an endoscopy. Coughing or choking with meals, especially after a stroke, needs a swallowing assessment.

Poor appetite and weight loss

Appetite often falls with age, but losing weight without trying is never normal.

What helps

  • Small, frequent meals with protein in each
  • Add dal, paneer, eggs, curd or milk to meals
  • Eat with family when possible
  • Ask your doctor to check teeth, mood and medicines

Losing weight without trying, for example 3 kg or more over 6 months, needs a check-up to find the cause.

Symptoms to take seriously after 60

These are often put down to age or piles. After 60 they need proper tests, because finding the cause early makes treatment simpler.

Blood in the stool

Piles are common, but after 60 any bleeding from the back passage needs a colonoscopy, because polyps and bowel cancer become more common. Black, tar-like stools mean bleeding higher up and need care the same day.

Anaemia

Low haemoglobin in an older person often comes from slow, hidden bleeding in the gut. It should be investigated, usually with an endoscopy and colonoscopy, and not only treated with iron tablets.

Jaundice, especially without pain

Yellow eyes with itching, dark urine and pale stools, especially without pain, can be a sign of a blocked bile duct from a stone or a growth. It needs urgent tests. An EUS or ERCP can often find the cause and relieve the blockage without surgery.

Gallstones and bile duct stones

Gallstones become more common with age. A stone that slips into the bile duct can cause pain, fever with chills and jaundice. This infection (cholangitis) is an emergency in older people, and the stone can usually be removed by ERCP.

Get emergency care if you or your parent has

  • Vomiting blood, or black, tar-like stools
  • Severe belly pain, or a hard, swollen belly
  • Fever with chills and yellow eyes
  • Vomiting with no stool or gas passing
  • Confusion, drowsiness or fainting with any gut symptom
  • Loose motions with dizziness or very little urine

Call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department. In older people, belly problems can be serious even when the pain seems mild.

If you have other health conditions

Most older people take medicines for other conditions. These affect the gut and how tests are planned.

Diabetes

Diabetes can slow stomach emptying, causing bloating and early fullness, and can cause loose motions or constipation. Some diabetes medicines also cause gut symptoms. Good sugar control helps, so tell your doctor about new symptoms.

Heart disease and blood thinners

Do not stop aspirin or blood thinners before an endoscopy or colonoscopy without advice from both your heart doctor and your gastroenterologist. Most procedures can be planned safely, and some can be done while you continue them.

Kidney and heart failure

If your doctor has limited fluids or salt, follow that advice over general tips on drinking water. Some laxatives and bowel preparations are not suitable, so check before using any.

What to eat after 60

Older people need as much protein as younger adults, but often eat less. Soft, home-cooked food with protein and fibre, and enough water, keeps the gut regular and the muscles strong.

Eat more

  • Protein at every meal: dal, paneer, curd, eggs, fish or chicken
  • Soft-cooked vegetables, and fruits such as papaya, banana and guava
  • Whole grains: dalia, oats, millets, atta with chokar
  • Water through the day, unless your doctor has limited fluids
  • Milk, curd or chaach for calcium

Cut down

  • Fried and very spicy food, especially at dinner
  • Extra salt and pickles, especially with high blood pressure
  • Sweets and sugary drinks, especially with diabetes
  • Alcohol
  • Large, late dinners

Good to know

  • Small, frequent meals are easier than three large ones
  • If chewing is hard, get dentures checked instead of skipping foods
  • Vitamin B12 and vitamin D are often low; ask your doctor about testing
  • Ask your doctor before taking herbal remedies or digestive powders, which can interact with medicines

A simple day of eating

Morning
Warm water, then tea with a few soaked almonds or walnuts
Breakfast
Vegetable dalia, upma, or besan chilla with curd
Mid-morning
A soft fruit such as papaya or banana
Lunch
Soft roti or rice, dal, a cooked vegetable and curd
Evening
Chaach or milk, with roasted chana or makhana
Dinner
Early and light: vegetable khichdi or dal and roti, 2 to 3 hours before bed

Tests, screening and endoscopy after 60

Age alone is not a reason to avoid an endoscopy or colonoscopy. They are safe in older people, with sedation adjusted to their health, and are often the quickest way to find and treat the cause.

Screening for bowel cancer is advised from 45 to 75 years. Discuss it with your doctor, especially if a close relative has had bowel cancer.

Bring a list of all your medicines, including blood thinners, diabetes medicines and herbal products, to every visit. Before a colonoscopy, the bowel preparation may need to be adjusted for diabetes, kidney or heart disease.

For families

If a parent has new gut symptoms, weight loss or anaemia, come together to the consultation if you can, and bring old reports and the full list of medicines. Video consultations are available for follow-ups and for reviewing reports.

Common questions

Is constipation normal in old age?

It is common, but should not be ignored. New constipation or a change in bowel habit after 60 should be checked.

Is endoscopy safe at 75 or 80?

Yes, for most people. Age alone is not a reason to avoid it. Heart, lung and kidney health are checked first, and sedation is adjusted.

Can I take painkillers for knee pain every day?

Regular use of painkillers such as ibuprofen or diclofenac can cause ulcers and bleeding. Ask your doctor about safer options and whether you need stomach protection.

My father has anaemia. Are iron tablets enough?

Iron treats the anaemia, but the cause must be found. In older people this usually means an endoscopy and colonoscopy.
