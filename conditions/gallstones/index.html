Gallbladder & bile ducts

Gallstones & bile duct stones

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Stones form in the gallbladder. Many cause no trouble. Some slip into the bile duct and block it, causing jaundice, infection or pancreatitis.

Common symptoms

  • Pain in the upper right or middle belly, often after a heavy meal
  • Pain spreading to the back or right shoulder
  • Jaundice, or fever with chills

Causes and risk factors

  • Being over 40
  • Women, especially after pregnancies
  • Extra weight or very rapid weight loss
  • Family history
  • Long fasts followed by heavy meals

How it is diagnosed

An ultrasound finds gallbladder stones. Liver tests, an MRCP scan or endoscopic ultrasound check for stones that have slipped into the bile duct.

Treatment

Gallbladder removal is done by a surgeon. Stones stuck in the bile duct are removed through ERCP, an endoscopic procedure with no cuts.

Silent stones or stones that cause trouble

Gallstones are very common in India, especially in North India and in women. Many are found by chance on an ultrasound done for another reason. These silent stones cause no pain, and most people who have them never develop symptoms.

Silent stones usually do not need an operation. In a few situations a surgeon may discuss removing the gallbladder even without pain, for example with very large stones or a gallbladder wall hardened with calcium (porcelain gallbladder). Because gallbladder cancer is more common in parts of North India, this may also come into the discussion. The decision is made person by person.

Once stones start causing pain, attacks tend to come back and the chance of complications rises. Removing the gallbladder is then usually advised.

A gallstone attack, and when it becomes serious

A typical attack (biliary colic) is a steady, severe pain in the upper middle or upper right belly, often after a heavy meal or at night. It may spread to the back or right shoulder blade, often comes with nausea or vomiting, and usually settles within a few hours. Bloating, gas and burping on their own are usually not due to gallstones.

Pain that lasts longer, or comes with fever or yellow eyes, suggests a complication:

  • Cholecystitis: an inflamed, often infected gallbladder, with pain lasting many hours and often fever.
  • Bile duct stones: a stone slips into the bile duct and blocks it, causing jaundice, dark urine and pale stools.
  • Cholangitis: infection behind a blocked duct, with fever, chills and jaundice. The duct needs to be cleared urgently, usually by ERCP.
  • Gallstone pancreatitis: severe pain going through to the back, with vomiting.

Pain lasting more than a few hours, pain with fever and chills, yellow eyes, or severe pain with repeated vomiting needs urgent care: call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Looking for stones in the bile duct

An ultrasound after a few hours of fasting finds gallbladder stones and shows whether the bile duct is widened. Liver function tests show whether bile flow is blocked. Doctors then judge how likely a duct stone is:

  • High chance (a stone seen in the duct, cholangitis, or jaundice with a widened duct): ERCP is usually done directly.
  • In-between chance (abnormal liver tests, a widened duct or older age): MRCP or EUS is done first, so that ERCP is used only when a stone is actually there.
  • Low chance (normal liver tests and a normal duct): the gallbladder can usually be removed without further duct tests.

MRCP is an MRI scan with no needle or tube. EUS, a thin endoscope with an ultrasound probe passed through the mouth under sedation, is especially good at finding small stones that MRCP can miss. If it finds one, ERCP can often follow in the same session.

Gallbladder removal and ERCP: what happens when

Laparoscopic (keyhole) cholecystectomy, done by a surgeon, is the standard operation for gallstones that cause symptoms. Most people go home within a day or two.

  • After attacks of pain: a planned operation, ideally before the next attack.
  • After cholecystitis: surgery during the same hospital stay, within the first few days, is generally preferred over waiting for weeks, if the person is fit.
  • After mild gallstone pancreatitis: the gallbladder is usually removed during the same admission.
  • With bile duct stones: the duct is usually cleared by ERCP before or around the time of the operation.

During ERCP, the opening of the bile duct is widened with a small cut and the stones are pulled out with a balloon or basket. Large stones may need crushing, sometimes over more than one session, with a temporary stent in between. ERCP needs no cuts on the skin but carries small risks, mainly pancreatitis, bleeding and infection.

ERCP does not remove the stones still in the gallbladder, so gallbladder removal is still advised afterwards for most people, unless they are very elderly or unfit for surgery.

Gallstones in pregnancy

Pregnancy hormones slow the gallbladder, so sludge and stones are more common during and soon after pregnancy. Ultrasound is safe at any stage, and MRCP without contrast or EUS can look for duct stones without X-rays. When surgery is needed, the middle three months are usually the safest time, though urgent surgery can be done at any stage. ERCP can be done when truly needed, keeping X-ray exposure very low. The obstetrician, surgeon and gastroenterologist decide together.

Myths and facts

Many people delay treatment because of things they have heard:

  • Home remedies, juices and powders do not melt gallstones, and some herbal products can harm the liver.
  • Oil and lemon "flushes" do not wash stones out. The soft lumps passed afterwards are formed from the oil itself.
  • Removing only the stones and keeping the gallbladder is not standard treatment, because new stones tend to form again.
  • Life without a gallbladder is normal. The liver keeps making bile, which flows straight into the intestine. Most people are back to their usual home food within a few weeks; pain that continues after surgery is worth getting checked.
  • Waiting does not make painful stones go away. It mainly raises the chance of a complication.

Diet with gallstones

Eat more

  • Regular meals, without long fasting gaps
  • Vegetables, fruits, dal and whole grains for fibre
  • Slow, steady weight loss if you are overweight

Cut down

  • Large, heavy, fried or very fatty meals, which can bring on pain
  • Crash diets and very rapid weight loss, which can form new stones
  • Home remedies that claim to flush or dissolve stones, such as oil and lemon 'cleanses'

Good to know

  • No diet dissolves gallstones. Stones that cause pain are treated by removing the gallbladder.
  • Stones found by chance, without pain, usually need no special diet

Common questions

Can gallstones be dissolved with medicine?

Rarely. Medicines work only for a few small stones and they often return, so surgery is the usual treatment for stones that cause pain.

Do silent gallstones need an operation?

Usually not. Stones found by chance that cause no symptoms are generally watched.
