Cirrhosis

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Years of liver injury replace healthy tissue with scar. Common causes are alcohol, fatty liver and hepatitis B or C.

Summary · the essentials in 2 minutes

What are the symptoms of cirrhosis?

  • Swelling of the belly or legs
  • Vomiting blood or black stools
  • Confusion or excessive sleepiness
  • Easy bruising

What causes cirrhosis?

  • Alcohol over many years
  • Fatty liver that has progressed
  • Hepatitis B or C
  • Autoimmune hepatitis
  • Rarer genetic and bile duct diseases

How is cirrhosis diagnosed?

Blood tests, an ultrasound and a liver stiffness test assess the liver. An endoscopy checks for enlarged veins (varices) in the food pipe, and an ultrasound every 6 months screens for liver cancer.

How is cirrhosis treated?

Treating the cause, screening for liver cancer every 6 months, and endoscopy to find and band enlarged veins (varices) before they bleed.

What should you eat with cirrhosis?

Eat more

  • Enough protein: dal, paneer, curd, eggs, fish or chicken
  • Small meals every 3 to 4 hours
  • A bedtime snack, such as milk with a roti or poha
  • Fresh home-cooked food

Cut down

  • Added salt, papad, pickles, namkeen and packaged foods, if you have swelling
  • Alcohol, completely
  • Raw or undercooked meat and seafood

Good to know

  • Do not cut protein unless your doctor tells you to
  • The bedtime snack helps protect muscle
  • With swelling: no more than 2 g of sodium a day (about one level teaspoon, 5 g, of salt in total), no low-sodium salt substitutes without advice, and fluids as your doctor says

When should you see a gastroenterologist for cirrhosis?

Anyone with cirrhosis needs regular specialist follow-up. Vomiting blood or confusion is an emergency.

Detailed guide

Cirrhosis: the detailed guide

For readers who want more than the summary: tests, treatment step by step, complications and follow-up. 6 sections · about 4 min read

1Compensated and decompensated cirrhosis

Cirrhosis has two broad stages, and the difference matters more than the word itself.

  • Compensated: the liver is scarred but still doing its work. Many people feel well and do not know they have it; it is often found on an ultrasound, a liver stiffness test or a low platelet count. People can stay in this stage for many years, especially when the cause is treated.
  • Decompensated: the liver can no longer keep up, and complications appear: fluid in the belly (ascites), bleeding from swollen veins (varices), confusion (hepatic encephalopathy) or jaundice. This stage needs closer care.

Doctors use scores such as Child-Pugh and MELD, worked out from blood tests and symptoms, to judge how advanced the cirrhosis is and to plan follow-up.

2What causes cirrhosis in India

Finding the cause matters, because treating it can stop further damage and, in some people, improve the liver. Many people have more than one cause.

  • Alcohol: one of the commonest causes. Stopping completely is the most important treatment, at any stage.
  • Fatty liver (MASLD): an increasingly common cause, often silent, in people with diabetes, obesity or high blood pressure.
  • Hepatitis B and C: hepatitis B is controlled with antiviral tablets and hepatitis C is curable; both reduce further damage.
  • Others: autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson's disease in young people, bile duct diseases, iron overload, and blocked veins draining the liver. Sometimes no cause is found.

3Complications and warning signs

Each complication has its own page on this site. In short:

  • Varices: high pressure in the liver's blood flow swells veins in the food pipe and stomach, which can bleed suddenly.
  • Ascites: fluid in the belly, sometimes with swollen legs. The fluid can get infected, causing fever or belly pain.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy: confusion, sleepiness, or changes in behaviour or handwriting. Constipation, infection, bleeding, dehydration and sleeping pills can bring it on.
  • Infections and kidney problems: infections are caught more easily, and the kidneys can be harmed by dehydration, infection or some painkillers.
  • Liver cancer: the risk is higher in cirrhosis, which is why regular ultrasound checks matter.
  • Muscle loss: common and often missed. Good nutrition helps prevent it.

Vomiting blood, black tarry stools, confusion or unusual sleepiness, or high fever are emergencies: call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

4Regular follow-up

Regular check-ups catch problems early, when they are easier to treat.

  • Ultrasound every 6 months, often with an AFP blood test, to find liver cancer at an early stage.
  • Endoscopy to look for varices, at diagnosis and then as the doctor advises. Depending on the findings, varices are treated with banding or with tablets that lower the pressure in these veins.
  • Blood tests every few months to check liver and kidney function, salts and blood counts.
  • Vaccines: hepatitis A and B if not already protected, a yearly flu vaccine and the pneumonia (pneumococcal) vaccine.

A liver transplant is discussed when cirrhosis has decompensated, when the MELD score rises, or with some early liver cancers. It is raised early, not as a last resort, because assessment takes time. In India, many transplants use part of a liver from a family member. Staying off alcohol is essential.

5Food: protein, salt and the late evening snack

A liver with cirrhosis stores little energy, so the body starts breaking down muscle during long gaps without food, especially overnight. Good nutrition protects muscle.

  • Eat small meals every 3 to 4 hours, and do not skip breakfast.
  • Have a late evening snack every night, such as milk with a roti, poha, or curd with a banana. It shortens the overnight fast.
  • Keep protein adequate: dal, paneer, curd, eggs, fish or chicken. Cutting protein causes muscle loss and is not advised, even after an episode of confusion, unless the doctor says so.
  • With fluid or swelling, keep total salt to about 5 g a day (about one level teaspoon, including the salt in cooking), and avoid papad, pickles, namkeen and packaged foods. Cutting salt much further is not advised, as food becomes tasteless and people eat less.
  • Do not use low-sodium salt substitutes unless the doctor agrees. They contain potassium, which can build up dangerously, especially with water tablets.
  • Drink fluids as the doctor advises. Most people do not need to limit water unless their blood sodium is low.

6Medicines and products to check first

A scarred liver handles medicines less well, and some common ones can tip it into trouble. Before taking anything new, tell the doctor or pharmacist that you have cirrhosis.

  • Anti-inflammatory painkillers, often bought over the counter for body ache, joint pain or headache: they can cause kidney failure, stomach bleeding and more fluid. Ask the doctor which pain relief is safer.
  • Sleeping pills, anti-anxiety tablets and other sedatives: they can bring on confusion.
  • Herbal and ayurvedic products, kadhas, 'liver tonics' and gym supplements: some damage the liver, and none has been shown to reverse cirrhosis.
  • Some blood pressure tablets and antibiotics may need changing; tell every doctor and dentist about the cirrhosis.

Do not stop prescribed medicines, such as water tablets or tablets for varices, without speaking to the doctor.

Common questions

Can cirrhosis be reversed?

Scarring usually does not fully go away, but treating the cause can stop it getting worse and sometimes improves it. In advanced cases, a liver transplant may be discussed.

Should I eat less protein?

No, unless your doctor advises it. Most people with cirrhosis need enough protein and a bedtime snack to protect their muscles.
