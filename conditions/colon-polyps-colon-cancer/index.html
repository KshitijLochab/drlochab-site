Colon polyps & colon cancer

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Polyps are small growths in the large bowel. Some turn into cancer over several years. Colon cancer is increasingly seen in people under 50.

Summary · the essentials in 2 minutes

Common symptoms

  • Blood in the stool
  • Change in bowel habit for more than 3 weeks
  • Anaemia, tiredness or weight loss
  • Often no symptoms in early stages

Causes and risk factors

  • Age over 45
  • A parent, brother or sister with colon cancer or polyps
  • Smoking and obesity
  • Red and processed meat, and low fibre
  • Long-standing ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease

How it is diagnosed

Colonoscopy is the best test: it finds polyps and removes them at the same time. A stool test for hidden blood (FIT) is another screening option.

Treatment

Colonoscopy finds polyps and removes them in the same test. Larger flat growths can often be removed endoscopically (EMR) without surgery.

Food that lowers colon cancer risk

Eat more

  • Fibre and whole grains: atta with chokar, millets, dal, vegetables and fruits
  • Milk, curd and other calcium-rich foods
  • Daily physical activity and a healthy waist size

Cut down

  • Processed meat, such as sausages, salami and ham, and large amounts of red meat
  • Alcohol and smoking
  • Sugary drinks, fried snacks and ultra-processed foods that add to weight gain

Good to know

  • Diet lowers the risk but does not replace screening; colonoscopy finds and removes polyps before they turn into cancer
  • During cancer treatment, keeping up weight and protein matters more than avoiding any one food

When to see a gastroenterologist

Any of these symptoms, or a parent, brother or sister with colon cancer.

Detailed guide

Colon polyps & colon cancer: the detailed guide

For readers who want more than the summary: tests, treatment step by step, complications and follow-up. 6 sections · about 4 min read

1What polyps are, and the types

A polyp is a small growth on the inner lining of the large bowel. Most are harmless, but some types can slowly turn into cancer, usually over about 10 years. Removing them during colonoscopy stops that change before it starts, which is why colonoscopy can prevent colon cancer, not just find it.

  • Adenomas: the commonest precancerous polyps. Larger ones, or those with certain features under the microscope, carry more risk.
  • Serrated polyps (sessile serrated lesions): flat, pale and easy to miss, often in the right side of the colon. They can also turn into cancer.
  • Hyperplastic polyps: usually small, in the lower bowel, and generally harmless.

The type cannot always be told by looking, so removed polyps are sent to the laboratory. The report decides when the next colonoscopy is due.

2How polyps are removed

Most polyps are removed during the same colonoscopy in which they are found, under sedation and without pain.

  • Small polyps: removed with a thin wire loop (snare) or small forceps.
  • Larger polyps: removed with a snare, sometimes using a mild electric current to seal the base.
  • Large flat polyps: removed by endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR). Fluid is injected under the polyp to lift it off the deeper wall, and it is then removed with a snare, in one piece or several. Clips may be placed to close the area.

Bleeding, sometimes a few days later, and rarely a small tear in the bowel are the main risks. Tell the doctor about blood thinners and diabetes medicines before the test. Growths that look like cancer are biopsied and marked, and may need surgery.

3When the next colonoscopy is due

The timing depends on what was found, how many polyps there were, their size and type, and how clean the bowel was. Your report will give a date. As a general guide for someone at average risk:

  • A normal, complete colonoscopy with a clean bowel: usually about 10 years later.
  • One or two small adenomas: often 7 to 10 years.
  • Several adenomas, or larger or higher-risk polyps: about 3 years, sometimes sooner.
  • A large polyp removed in pieces: a check of the site in about 6 months.

If the bowel was not clean enough, the test usually needs to be repeated within a year.

4Who should be screened, and when

  • Average risk: screening from about 45, up to about 75.
  • A parent, brother, sister or child with colon cancer or an advanced polyp: colonoscopy from 40, or 10 years before the age at which that relative was diagnosed, whichever comes first, and usually repeated more often.
  • Families with many members with bowel, womb or other cancers, especially at young ages, or with hundreds of polyps: genetic advice is needed, and checks may begin in the teenage years.
  • Long-standing ulcerative colitis or Crohn's colitis: regular surveillance colonoscopy.

Doctors in India are seeing colon and rectal cancer in people in their 30s and 40s more often than before. Young age is not a reason to assume bleeding is only piles.

5Stool tests or colonoscopy?

A stool test for hidden blood (FIT) is simple and done at home. It is repeated every year, and a positive result needs a colonoscopy. It can miss many polyps and cannot remove them. A stool test is for people without symptoms; anyone with symptoms needs a colonoscopy.

Colonoscopy looks at the whole colon and removes polyps in the same sitting. Its value depends on a clean bowel: small flat polyps hide under stool. Following the bowel preparation instructions carefully, including the diet the day before and taking the solution in split doses, makes the test safer and more accurate. The colonoscopy preparation guide on this site explains it step by step.

6Warning signs

These symptoms need a check at any age, even if you have had piles before:

  • Blood in the stool, or black stools.
  • A change in bowel habit lasting more than 3 weeks, such as new constipation, loose motions or thinner stools.
  • Anaemia, tiredness or weight loss without a clear reason.
  • Belly pain or a lump that does not go away, or a feeling of not emptying fully.

Heavy bleeding from the back passage, fainting, or severe belly pain with a swollen belly and vomiting needs urgent care: call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Common questions

When should I get a colonoscopy?

Discuss it with your doctor from about 45, or about 10 years earlier than the age at which a close relative was diagnosed. Symptoms need testing at any age.

Does polyp removal hurt?

No. The bowel lining has no pain nerves, and the procedure is done under sedation.
