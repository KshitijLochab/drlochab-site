Ulcerative colitis & Crohn's disease

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

In inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) the immune system inflames the bowel. It is becoming more common in India and is often first mistaken for an infection or TB.

Common symptoms

  • Loose motions with blood or mucus for weeks
  • Belly pain and weight loss
  • Urgency, or waking at night to pass stool

Causes and risk factors

  • The immune system attacking the bowel lining
  • Family history
  • Environmental factors such as diet and smoking (for Crohn's)
  • Not caused by stress or food alone, though both can trigger flares

How it is diagnosed

Colonoscopy with biopsies confirms it. Stool calprotectin, blood tests and MRI or CT scans of the small bowel show how active it is. Intestinal TB is ruled out before starting some treatments.

Treatment

Colonoscopy with biopsy confirms it. Modern medicines control the inflammation and keep most people well for long periods.

Ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease: how they differ

Both are types of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Both come in flares, with quieter spells in between, and both can also affect the joints, eyes, skin or liver.

  • Ulcerative colitis affects only the large bowel. It starts at the rectum and spreads upwards in one continuous stretch, and it inflames only the inner lining. The main symptoms are loose motions with blood and mucus, and urgency.
  • Crohn's disease can affect any part of the gut from mouth to anus, most often the end of the small bowel and the colon. It comes in patches, with normal bowel in between, and can go through the full thickness of the bowel wall. It may cause belly pain, weight loss, narrowing (strictures), abnormal tunnels (fistulas), and boils or fistulas around the anus.

In teenagers, Crohn's disease can slow growth and delay puberty, so height and weight are checked at every visit.

How the diagnosis is made

There is no single test for IBD. The diagnosis is built from symptoms, colonoscopy, biopsies and scans.

  • Colonoscopy with biopsies from several places, including the end of the small bowel, shows the pattern of inflammation.
  • Stool tests: calprotectin shows inflammation in the bowel and is also used later to check whether treatment is working. Stool tests for infection are done during a flare.
  • Blood tests: blood count, CRP, albumin, iron and vitamin levels.
  • For Crohn's disease: MR or CT enterography scans to see the small bowel, and sometimes capsule endoscopy or an upper GI endoscopy.

In India, intestinal tuberculosis can look almost exactly like Crohn's disease. TB must be ruled out with biopsy tests for TB, a chest X-ray or CT and other tests before Crohn's treatment starts, because steroids and strong immune medicines can make undiagnosed TB much worse. When the picture is still unclear, the doctor may first give a full course of TB treatment and check the response.

Treatment goals and medicines

Treatment has three aims: to control the flare, to keep the disease in remission, and to heal the lining of the bowel. Feeling better is the first step; healing seen on colonoscopy and a normal calprotectin lower the chance of future flares, hospital stays and surgery.

  • Aminosalicylates: tablets, and enemas or suppositories for the rectum. They are the main treatment for mild to moderate ulcerative colitis.
  • Steroids: used only as short courses to settle a flare, never as long-term treatment, because of side effects such as bone thinning, high sugar, weight gain and infections.
  • Immune-modifying tablets: older and newer tablets that calm the overactive immune system and keep the disease quiet.
  • Biologic injections or drips: given under the skin or into a vein at fixed intervals, for moderate to severe disease or when tablets are not enough.

Inflammation can continue silently even when you feel well. Stopping medicines on your own once symptoms settle is one of the commonest reasons for a flare, and a flare after stopping is sometimes harder to control. Any change should be discussed with your gastroenterologist first. In Crohn's disease, stopping smoking is as important as any medicine. Avoid anti-inflammatory painkillers for body pain without advice.

Infections, vaccines and pregnancy

Before strong immune medicines start, a few checks help prevent serious infections:

  • Screening for TB, hepatitis B, hepatitis C and HIV, with blood tests and a chest X-ray.
  • Vaccines: a yearly flu vaccine, pneumonia vaccine, hepatitis B vaccine if not immune, and others according to age. Live vaccines are generally avoided while on strong immune medicines, so they are planned before treatment begins.

Women with IBD can usually have a normal pregnancy. The right time to conceive is when the disease has been in remission for some months. Most IBD medicines are continued during pregnancy, because a flare carries more risk for the baby than the medicine does. A few medicines must be stopped well before trying to conceive, so plan pregnancy with your gastroenterologist and obstetrician, and never stop treatment on finding out you are pregnant without asking.

Long-term checks and when surgery helps

Long-standing colitis raises the risk of colon cancer. Anyone whose ulcerative colitis or Crohn's colitis involves more than the rectum is advised a surveillance colonoscopy about 8 years after diagnosis, and then every 1 to 3 years depending on the findings. People who also have primary sclerosing cholangitis, a bile duct disease linked with colitis, start at diagnosis and are checked every year.

Surgery is part of good care, not a failure of treatment:

  • In ulcerative colitis, removing the colon is advised for a severe flare that does not respond to medicines, or for cancer or precancerous change. It removes the disease from the bowel, and an internal pouch made from the small bowel often avoids a permanent bag.
  • In Crohn's disease, surgery helps with strictures, fistulas and abscesses that medicines cannot control. It does not cure Crohn's, so the surgeon removes as little bowel as possible and medicines continue afterwards. Some short narrowings can be stretched with a balloon during colonoscopy instead.

Warning signs of a severe flare

A severe flare can become dangerous within days and usually needs hospital care. Seek help the same day if you have:

  • Six or more bloody stools a day.
  • Fever, a fast pulse, dizziness or fainting.
  • A swollen, tight or very painful belly, or vomiting.
  • Being unable to eat or drink, or passing very little urine.

If these are severe or getting worse quickly, call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Diet in ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease

Eat more

  • When well: a varied home diet with vegetables, fruits, dal, whole grains, curd, eggs and fish
  • During a flare: soft, well-cooked food in small meals, such as khichdi, curd rice, idli, soups and cooked vegetables
  • Enough protein and calories, especially if you are losing weight

Cut down

  • Ultra-processed foods: packaged snacks, processed meat and drinks with many additives
  • Whole corn, seeds, tough peels and other fibrous chunks if you have a narrowing (stricture), as advised
  • Painkillers for body pain without advice, which can trigger flares

Good to know

  • Diet supports treatment but does not replace medicines
  • Iron, vitamin D, calcium and B12 are often low; your doctor will check them
  • Avoid cutting out many foods at once; a dietitian helps if you are losing weight

Common questions

Is IBD the same as IBS?

No. IBD causes real inflammation and damage in the bowel; IBS does not. They are treated very differently.

Can people with IBD lead a normal life?

Yes. With regular treatment, most people work, travel and have families. Pregnancy should be planned with your doctor.
