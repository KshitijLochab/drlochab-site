Summary · the essentials in 2 minutes

What are the symptoms of pancreatitis?

  • Severe upper belly pain going through to the back
  • Vomiting
  • In chronic disease: oily stools, weight loss, diabetes

What causes pancreatitis?

  • Gallstones
  • Alcohol
  • Very high triglycerides
  • Some medicines
  • Smoking, and in chronic pancreatitis, genetic factors and tropical pancreatitis

How is pancreatitis diagnosed?

Blood amylase or lipase and an ultrasound confirm an attack. A CT scan, MRCP or endoscopic ultrasound shows complications and the cause.

How is pancreatitis treated?

Acute attacks need hospital care. Fluid collections, duct stones and narrowings can often be treated with EUS or ERCP instead of open surgery.

What should you eat with pancreatitis?

Eat more

  • In the first few weeks: small, frequent, lighter meals
  • Boiled, steamed or lightly cooked food
  • Protein: dal, curd, paneer, eggs, fish, chicken
  • Plenty of fluids

Cut down

  • Alcohol, completely and for good
  • Smoking
  • Fried food and very large meals
  • Sugar, sweets and sweet drinks if triglycerides or blood sugar are high

Good to know

  • After a single attack, most people can return to a normal, balanced diet once recovered
  • In chronic pancreatitis, do not cut fat drastically; enzyme capsules with meals help digest it
  • If gallstones caused the attack, removing the gallbladder prevents another
  • Sudden severe pain with vomiting needs emergency care

Detailed guide

Pancreatitis: the detailed guide

For readers who want more than the summary: tests, treatment step by step, complications and follow-up. 6 sections · about 5 min read

1Acute or chronic, and what causes it

Acute pancreatitis is a sudden inflammation of the pancreas. Most people recover fully. Chronic pancreatitis is lasting damage with scarring, which slowly affects digestion and blood sugar.

The common causes in India are:

  • Gallstones: a small stone blocks the shared opening of the bile and pancreatic ducts. Along with alcohol, it is one of the two commonest causes.
  • Alcohol: usually after years of regular heavy drinking. Smoking adds to the risk.
  • Very high triglycerides (a blood fat), often with uncontrolled diabetes, alcohol, obesity or pregnancy.
  • Some medicines, which the doctor will review one by one.
  • After ERCP: a known risk of the procedure. Most such attacks are mild.
  • Less common: an injury to the belly, high blood calcium, gene changes, or problems in the shape of the pancreatic duct.

When no cause is found, EUS or MRCP can look for tiny stones (sludge) or a duct problem. In people over about 40, doctors also make sure a small growth is not blocking the duct.

2How doctors judge how serious an attack is

Most attacks are mild and settle within a few days. Severity is judged over the first two days. A very high amylase or lipase level does not mean a worse attack.

  • Mild: no organ failure and no complications around the pancreas. Most people go home within a few days to a week.
  • Moderately severe: fluid collections or damaged tissue around the pancreas, or strain on an organ that recovers within 48 hours.
  • Severe: failure of one or more organs, such as the lungs, kidneys or circulation, lasting more than 48 hours. This needs intensive care.

Doctors closely watch pulse, blood pressure, breathing, oxygen and urine output, and repeat blood tests. Most people do not need a CT scan in the first days; it is done if the diagnosis is uncertain or there is no improvement after two to three days.

3Care in hospital, in plain words

There is no medicine that switches off pancreatitis. Treatment supports the body while the pancreas heals.

  • Fluids through a drip: given in measured amounts in the first one to two days and adjusted to pulse, blood pressure and urine output. Both too little and too much can harm.
  • Pain relief: proper pain relief is part of treatment.
  • Early feeding: in mild attacks, eating can start as soon as the pain eases and vomiting stops, usually within a day or two, with a light, low-fat diet. There is no need to wait for blood tests to become normal.
  • In severe attacks: food is often given through a thin tube from the nose into the stomach or intestine, which is better than feeding through a vein.
  • Antibiotics are not given routinely. They are used only when there is an infection, such as cholangitis or infected dead tissue.
  • Gallstone pancreatitis: an urgent ERCP is needed only if there is cholangitis or the bile duct stays blocked. Otherwise the duct is checked later if needed.

4Preventing another attack

Pancreatitis often comes back if its cause remains. Treating the cause is the most important step after recovery.

  • Gallstones: removing the gallbladder prevents further gallstone attacks. After a mild attack it is usually done during the same admission. After a severe attack with fluid collections, the surgeon may wait until things settle. In people who cannot have surgery, ERCP with a small cut in the duct opening lowers the risk.
  • Alcohol: stop completely and for good; cutting down is not enough. Help to stop drinking ideally starts while still in hospital.
  • Smoking: stopping lowers the risk of repeated attacks and of chronic pancreatitis.
  • High triglycerides: diet, weight control, good diabetes control, no alcohol and, usually, triglyceride-lowering medicine, with regular blood checks.
  • Medicines: a medicine suspected of causing the attack is stopped or changed by the doctor. Do not stop prescribed medicines on your own.

5Fluid collections, necrosis and diabetes

Some attacks leave fluid or dead (necrotic) tissue around the pancreas. Early collections often go away on their own. Those that remain after about four weeks develop a wall and are called a pseudocyst or walled-off necrosis.

Most collections need no procedure. Drainage is considered when a collection is infected, causes pain, vomiting or jaundice, or keeps growing. When possible, doctors wait until the wall has formed and then drain it with EUS through the stomach wall, using a stent. The page on pseudocysts and walled-off necrosis explains more.

Diabetes can develop after acute pancreatitis, even after a single attack, and more often after severe or repeated attacks. A blood sugar check (fasting sugar or HbA1c) in the months after recovery, and then at regular intervals as the doctor advises, is sensible.

Oily, foul-smelling stools, or weight loss despite eating well, can mean the pancreas is not making enough digestive enzymes. Enzyme capsules taken with meals help, so tell your doctor if this happens.

6When to call 112

Pancreatitis can worsen quickly, especially in the first two days. Seek emergency help without delay for:

  • Sudden, severe upper belly pain going through to the back, especially with repeated vomiting.
  • Breathlessness, confusion, passing very little urine, or feeling faint, cold and clammy.
  • Fever with chills, or yellow eyes, after a recent attack.
  • Vomiting blood or passing black stools.

Do not wait at home for the pain to settle. Call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Common questions

Can pancreatitis come back?

Yes, if the cause continues. Removing the gallbladder after gallstone pancreatitis and stopping alcohol and smoking greatly reduce the risk.

Can pancreatitis cause diabetes?

Yes. Repeated damage in chronic pancreatitis can reduce insulin, so blood sugar is checked regularly.
