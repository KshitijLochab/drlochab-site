Pancreatic cysts & lumps

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Cysts in the pancreas are often found on a scan done for another reason. Most are harmless. Some need monitoring or treatment.

Summary · the essentials in 2 minutes

Common symptoms

  • Often no symptoms
  • When present: upper belly pain, jaundice or weight loss

Causes and risk factors

  • Fluid collections (pseudocysts) after pancreatitis
  • Serous cysts, which are almost always harmless
  • Mucinous cysts, some of which can change over time
  • Cysts linked to the pancreatic duct (IPMN)

How it is diagnosed

A CT or MRI scan shows the cyst. Endoscopic ultrasound gives a closer view and can sample the fluid with a fine needle to identify the type.

Treatment

Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) gives a close view and can take a fine-needle sample, so the right plan can be made.

Diet with a pancreatic cyst

Eat more

  • Normal, balanced home food; most cysts need no special diet
  • Vegetables, fruits and whole grains
  • A healthy weight and good blood sugar control

Cut down

  • Alcohol
  • Smoking, which raises the risk of pancreatic cancer
  • Sugary drinks and sweets if blood sugar is rising

Good to know

  • Diet does not change the size of a cyst; keep your follow-up scans
  • New diabetes, weight loss, jaundice or oily stools should be reported early

When to see a gastroenterologist

Any cyst or lump in the pancreas reported on a scan.

Pancreatic cysts & lumps: the detailed guide

For readers who want more than the summary: tests, treatment step by step, complications and follow-up. 6 sections · about 4 min read

1A common finding, and usually not cancer

Pancreatic cysts are small fluid-filled sacs in the pancreas. With more CT and MRI scans being done for other reasons, they are found very often, and more often with age. Most are harmless and never cause any problem.

Finding a cyst is a reason for a careful plan, not for alarm. The aim is to pick out the few cysts that may turn into cancer over years, without putting everyone else through unnecessary tests or surgery.

2The main types, in plain words

  • Pseudocyst: fluid left behind after pancreatitis. It is not a growth and does not turn into cancer. Many shrink on their own; some need drainage, as explained on the pseudocyst page.
  • Serous cyst (serous cystadenoma): often looks like a honeycomb on the scan. It is almost always harmless and usually needs no long-term follow-up unless it causes symptoms.
  • Mucinous cystic neoplasm: seen mostly in women, usually in the body or tail of the pancreas. It can turn into cancer over years, so removal is often discussed, depending on its size and features.
  • IPMN (intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm): a cyst that grows from the duct system of the pancreas. The common branch-duct type is usually low risk and is watched with scans. The less common type that involves the main duct carries a higher risk, and surgery is usually discussed.
  • Rarer types, including some with solid areas, are reviewed by a specialist team.

Telling the types apart from a scan alone is not always possible. The person's age and sex, any past pancreatitis, and the cyst's location and appearance all help.

3Features that need a closer look

Most cysts can be followed with MRI scans. EUS, and a review by a specialist team, is advised when any of these is present:

  • A solid part or a nodule inside the cyst.
  • A widened main pancreatic duct, or a sudden change in the width of the duct.
  • A large cyst, about 3 cm or more, or a cyst that keeps growing between scans.
  • Jaundice, or an attack of pancreatitis caused by the cyst.
  • New or worsening diabetes, or weight loss without a reason.
  • A raised CA 19-9 (a blood tumour marker), when it has been checked.

Severe upper belly pain with vomiting, or jaundice with fever, needs emergency care: call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

4What EUS with fine-needle sampling adds

In EUS, a thin endoscope with an ultrasound probe at its tip is passed through the mouth under sedation. From inside the stomach and duodenum it lies right next to the pancreas, and gives a much closer view than a CT or MRI.

  • It shows fine details: thin walls inside the cyst, a solid nodule, and whether the cyst connects with the pancreatic duct.
  • A fine needle can draw out cyst fluid through the wall of the stomach or duodenum, with no cut on the skin.
  • The fluid is tested for abnormal cells and for markers such as CEA and sugar (glucose), which help tell mucinous from non-mucinous cysts. Some centres also test it for gene changes.
  • A solid area can be sampled for a tissue diagnosis.

Most people go home the same day. The risks are small and include pancreatitis, infection and bleeding. EUS is not needed for every cyst; it is used when its result would change the plan.

5Follow-up scans: how often and for how long

Small cysts without worrying features are usually followed with MRI, often with MRCP, which uses no radiation. The gap between scans depends on the cyst's size and type. Smaller cysts are checked less often, and the gap may be lengthened if a cyst stays the same over several years.

Not every cyst needs lifelong scans. Serous cysts with a clear diagnosis, and pseudocysts that have settled, usually need no long-term follow-up. Scans are also stopped when a person would not be fit for surgery, or would not want it, because the result would no longer change treatment.

6When surgery is discussed

Surgery is discussed when there is a solid part, an IPMN involving the main duct, cancer or high-risk changes on sampling, jaundice, or symptoms clearly caused by the cyst. Pancreatic surgery is a major operation, so the decision is made by a team of gastroenterologist, surgeon and radiologist, weighing the person's age, overall health and wishes.

Between scans, do not smoke, keep alcohol to a minimum and keep blood sugar in check. Report jaundice, new diabetes, weight loss or oily stools early rather than waiting for the next scan.

Common questions

Is a pancreatic cyst cancer?

Most are not. A few types can turn into cancer over years, which is why the type and size decide whether to watch it or treat it.

How often do I need scans?

It depends on the cyst type and size. Small, harmless-looking cysts may need a scan every one to two years.
