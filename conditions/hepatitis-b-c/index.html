Hepatitis B & C

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Viral infections spread through blood, unsafe injections, unscreened transfusions, sexual contact (mainly hepatitis B) and from mother to baby. Many people carry them for years without symptoms.

Summary · the essentials in 2 minutes

What are the symptoms of hepatitis B and C?

  • Often no symptoms
  • Found during blood donation, pregnancy or pre-surgery tests
  • Later: jaundice, swelling of the legs or belly

What causes hepatitis B and C?

  • Unsafe injections and reused needles
  • Blood transfusions that were not screened
  • Mother to baby at birth (mainly hepatitis B)
  • Unprotected sexual contact (mainly hepatitis B)
  • Tattoos, piercing or dental work with unsterile tools

How is hepatitis B and C diagnosed?

HBsAg and anti-HCV blood tests screen for infection. Viral load tests (HBV DNA, HCV RNA), liver tests and a liver stiffness test decide whether and how to treat.

How is hepatitis B and C treated?

Hepatitis C is curable with a short course of tablets. Hepatitis B can be controlled with medicines and regular monitoring, and is preventable with vaccination.

What should you eat with hepatitis B and C?

Eat more

  • Normal, balanced home food; no special diet is needed
  • Vegetables, fruits, dal and whole grains to keep weight in a healthy range
  • Plain coffee, if you like it, which is linked to less liver scarring

Cut down

  • Alcohol, which speeds up liver damage
  • Herbal, Ayurvedic or 'liver tonic' products without asking your doctor
  • Sugar, maida and fried food, which add fatty liver on top of hepatitis

Good to know

  • Hepatitis B and C do not spread through sharing food, utensils or water
  • Ask about hepatitis A vaccination, as another infection can harm a liver that is already affected

When should you see a gastroenterologist for hepatitis B and C?

Any positive HBsAg or anti-HCV test, even if you feel completely well.

Detailed guide

Hepatitis B & C: the detailed guide

For readers who want more than the summary: tests, treatment step by step, complications and follow-up. 6 sections · about 5 min read

1Reading the reports in plain words

What the common codes on a hepatitis report mean:

  • HBsAg: positive means the hepatitis B virus is in the body now. If it stays positive for more than 6 months, it is called chronic hepatitis B.
  • Anti-HBs: antibodies that protect against hepatitis B. Positive, with a negative HBsAg, means the person is protected, by vaccination or by an old infection that has cleared.
  • Anti-HBc: shows the person has met the hepatitis B virus at some time. It stays positive for life and matters before chemotherapy or immune-lowering treatment.
  • HBeAg: usually goes with a high amount of virus. Many adults are HBeAg-negative, but that alone does not mean the virus is quiet.
  • HBV DNA: the viral load, the amount of hepatitis B virus in the blood.
  • Anti-HCV: shows exposure to hepatitis C at some time. It stays positive even after the virus clears or is cured.
  • HCV RNA: shows whether hepatitis C is active now. Positive means a current infection that needs treatment.

Normal SGPT and SGOT do not rule out hepatitis B or C. A liver stiffness test (FibroScan or similar) measures scarring.

2Chronic hepatitis B: phases, and who needs treatment

Chronic hepatitis B moves through phases over the years. Treatment depends on the viral load, liver enzymes, the amount of scarring, age and family history, not on HBsAg alone.

  • High virus, normal liver tests (once called the 'immune tolerant' phase): common in young people infected at birth. Often monitored without treatment.
  • High virus with raised liver tests (chronic hepatitis, HBeAg-positive or negative): the virus is damaging the liver, and treatment is usually advised.
  • Low virus, normal liver tests (once called the 'inactive carrier' state): the commonest pattern in adults. The outlook is usually good, and regular monitoring is enough.

Treatment is also advised for anyone with cirrhosis and detectable virus, and to protect people starting chemotherapy or strong immune-lowering medicines. It means antiviral tablets taken daily, usually for many years. They keep the virus suppressed and lower the risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer, but rarely clear HBsAg completely.

  • Never stop hepatitis B tablets on your own. Stopping suddenly can cause a severe flare of hepatitis.

People not on treatment still need regular liver tests and viral load checks, because the phase can change.

3Hepatitis C: curable with a short course of tablets

Hepatitis C can now be cured in most people with a short course of antiviral tablets, usually with mild side effects.

  • Before treatment: an HCV RNA test confirms active infection, and a hepatitis B test is done, because hepatitis B can flare during hepatitis C treatment. Tell the doctor about all medicines and supplements, as some interact with the tablets.
  • After treatment: a repeat HCV RNA test confirms the cure. Anti-HCV stays positive for life; this is expected and does not mean the infection is back.
  • Cure does not protect against a new infection, so avoid shared needles, razors and unsafe injections.

People who had cirrhosis or advanced scarring before the cure still need liver cancer checks every 6 months. The risk falls but does not disappear.

4Pregnancy and babies

A hepatitis B test is part of routine pregnancy tests, and testing for hepatitis C is also advised.

  • Mother with hepatitis B: the baby should get the hepatitis B vaccine and hepatitis B immunoglobulin soon after birth, ideally within 12 hours, and then complete the routine vaccine course. This protects most babies.
  • If the mother's viral load is high, antiviral tablets are offered in the later part of pregnancy to lower the risk to the baby.
  • Hepatitis B is not by itself a reason for a caesarean section. Breastfeeding is safe once the baby has had the vaccine and immunoglobulin.
  • Mother with hepatitis C: hepatitis C tablets are not routinely given in pregnancy, so treatment is planned before pregnancy or after delivery. Breastfeeding is generally safe unless the nipples are cracked or bleeding, and the baby is tested later.

5Protecting the family, and liver cancer checks

Hepatitis B often runs in families, passing from mother to child and between partners.

  • Testing: the spouse or partner, children, parents, and brothers and sisters of a person with hepatitis B should be tested for HBsAg and anti-HBs.
  • Vaccination: anyone not infected and not already protected should take the hepatitis B vaccine course. Partners have an antibody test afterwards, and use condoms until protected.
  • Hepatitis C: there is no vaccine. Family members with the same risks, such as old injections or transfusions, and children of a mother with hepatitis C should be tested.

People with hepatitis B or C and cirrhosis need an ultrasound every 6 months, often with an AFP blood test, to find liver cancer early. Some people with hepatitis B need them even without cirrhosis, such as those with a family history of liver cancer; the doctor decides from age, sex and other risks.

6Living a normal life

Hepatitis B and C do not spread through sharing food, utensils, water or toilets, or through hugging, coughing or sneezing. People with hepatitis B or C can work, study, play sports, marry and have children.

  • Do not share razors, toothbrushes, nail cutters or needles. Cover cuts and wipe up blood spills with bleach.
  • Do not donate blood.
  • Avoid alcohol, keep a healthy weight, and ask your doctor before any herbal product or new medicine.
  • Tell every doctor and dentist that you have hepatitis B or C.

Being open with a partner, and getting them tested and vaccinated, protects both.

Common questions

Is hepatitis C curable?

Yes. A short course of tablets cures most people. A blood test after the course confirms the cure.

Can someone with hepatitis B marry and have children?

Yes. The partner should be tested and vaccinated, and the baby gets the vaccine and immunoglobulin at birth, which protects most babies.
