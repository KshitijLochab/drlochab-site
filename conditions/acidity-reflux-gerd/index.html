Food pipe & stomach

Acidity & reflux (GERD)

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Stomach acid flows back up into the food pipe. Occasional heartburn is common. Frequent reflux can inflame the food pipe and, over years, damage its lining.

Summary · the essentials in 2 minutes

Common symptoms

  • Burning behind the chest after meals
  • Sour taste or food coming back up
  • Worse when lying down or bending
  • Long-standing cough or hoarse voice

Causes and risk factors

  • A weak valve between the food pipe and stomach
  • Large or late meals, and lying down soon after eating
  • Extra weight, especially around the belly
  • Smoking, alcohol, and some medicines
  • Pregnancy and hiatus hernia

How it is diagnosed

Most people need no tests at first. An upper GI endoscopy is done if symptoms continue despite treatment, or if there are warning signs such as trouble swallowing, weight loss, vomiting blood or anaemia.

Treatment

Smaller meals, dinner at least 3 hours before bed, raising the head end of the bed, losing extra weight, and cutting down on tobacco and alcohol. Medicines work well when needed.

What to eat: Acidity & reflux

Eat more

  • Smaller meals, more often
  • Dinner at least 3 hours before bed
  • Oats, cooked vegetables, non-citrus fruits like banana and papaya
  • Water between meals
  • A short walk after eating

Cut down

  • Large, late dinners
  • Fried and very fatty food
  • Too much tea, coffee and fizzy drinks
  • Chocolate and mint, which are common triggers
  • Alcohol and tobacco

Good to know

  • Losing weight, if you are overweight, is one of the most effective steps
  • Triggers differ from person to person; a two-week food diary shows yours
  • Raise the head end of the bed by 6 to 8 inches for night symptoms
  • Trouble swallowing or weight loss needs a specialist visit

When to see a gastroenterologist

Symptoms twice a week or more, no relief after 2 to 4 weeks, or any warning sign such as trouble swallowing or weight loss.

Detailed guide

Acidity & reflux (GERD): the detailed guide

For readers who want more than the summary: tests, treatment step by step, complications and follow-up. 5 sections · about 3 min read

1Heartburn, reflux or something else?

Heartburn is a burning feeling behind the breastbone, usually after meals or on lying down. Reflux means stomach contents coming back up into the food pipe or mouth. Many people with reflux also have a sour taste, burping, a feeling of food stuck in the chest, or a cough and hoarse voice in the morning.

Not every burning or upper belly pain is reflux. Indigestion (dyspepsia), gallstones, ulcers and, importantly, the heart can cause similar discomfort.

  • Chest pain that comes on with walking or climbing stairs, spreads to the arm, jaw or back, or comes with sweating or breathlessness needs emergency care, not an antacid. Call 112.

2Tests, when they are needed

Most people with typical heartburn and no warning signs can start treatment without any test. Tests help when the diagnosis is uncertain, treatment is not working, or there are warning signs.

  • Upper GI endoscopy: looks at the food pipe and stomach for inflammation (oesophagitis), a narrowing, a hiatus hernia, Barrett's oesophagus, ulcers or growths. It is advised for difficulty swallowing, vomiting, weight loss, anaemia, bleeding, or new symptoms after the age of about 45 to 50.
  • 24-hour pH or pH-impedance study: a thin tube or a small capsule measures how often acid comes up. It confirms reflux before long-term treatment or surgery, and when symptoms do not respond to tablets.
  • Oesophageal manometry: measures how the food pipe muscles squeeze. It is done before anti-reflux surgery and when swallowing is difficult.

3Treatment, step by step

Treatment is built in steps, and most people need only the first two.

  • Daily habits: smaller meals, dinner 2 to 3 hours before lying down, raising the head end of the bed by 6 to 8 inches (blocks under the legs of the bed, not extra pillows) if symptoms come at night, losing extra weight, and stopping smoking. Cut down on foods that clearly trigger your symptoms; there is no need to avoid foods that do not.
  • Occasional symptoms: antacids or alginate liquids give quick relief for a few hours.
  • Frequent symptoms: acid-reducing tablets, usually a course of about 8 weeks taken before breakfast. If symptoms settle, the doctor will try to reduce or stop the tablets, or use them only when needed.
  • Symptoms that do not settle: the next step is to check that the diagnosis is right with an endoscopy and a pH study, rather than simply adding more tablets.
  • Surgery or endoscopic procedures: for selected people with proven reflux who do not want long-term tablets or whose symptoms continue, anti-reflux surgery (fundoplication) or, in some centres, endoscopic anti-reflux procedures may be offered after tests.

Acid-reducing tablets are safe for most people when there is a clear reason to take them. Taking them for years without a reason is not advised; your doctor will review whether you still need them.

4Complications to know about

Most reflux causes discomfort but no lasting harm. Over years, frequent reflux can lead to:

  • Oesophagitis: inflammation and sores in the food pipe, which can bleed.
  • Stricture: scarring that narrows the food pipe and makes swallowing difficult. It can usually be stretched with an endoscope.
  • Barrett's oesophagus: a change in the lining that slightly raises the risk of food pipe cancer. People who have it are offered regular endoscopy checks.

5Reflux in pregnancy

Heartburn is very common in pregnancy, especially in the last three months, as hormones relax the valve and the growing baby presses on the stomach. Small frequent meals, not lying down after eating and raising the head end of the bed help most women. Some antacids are considered safe in pregnancy, but check with your obstetrician before taking any medicine.

Common questions

Can acidity medicines be taken for a long time?

They are safe for most people when needed, but long-term use should be reviewed by a doctor, who will usually aim for the lowest dose that controls symptoms.

Can acidity feel like heart pain?

Yes, and the two can be hard to tell apart. Chest pain with sweating, breathlessness, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw must be checked for heart problems first, as an emergency.
