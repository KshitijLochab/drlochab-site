Why do I get so much gas and bloating?

By Dr. Kshitij Lochab, Consultant Gastroenterologist · Medically reviewed by Dr. Kshitij Lochab on 31 October 2026

Share on WhatsApp

Short answer

Gas and bloating are very common and are usually not a sign of serious disease. Swallowed air, some foods, constipation and a sensitive gut (IBS) are the usual causes. See a doctor if it is new, keeps getting worse, or comes with weight loss, vomiting or blood in the stool.

Common causes

  • Eating fast, talking while eating, chewing gum and fizzy drinks, which add swallowed air
  • Foods that make more gas in the gut, such as rajma, chana, cabbage and onion, or a lot of new fibre at once
  • Milk and milk products, in people who do not digest lactose well
  • Constipation, which traps gas behind hard stool
  • IBS, where the gut feels normal amounts of gas more strongly

What usually helps

  • Eat slowly, at regular times, and do not lie down right after meals
  • Cut down on fizzy drinks and chewing gum
  • Keep a food diary for a week to find your own triggers, instead of stopping many foods at once
  • Add fibre slowly and drink enough water
  • A 15-minute walk after meals helps gas move along

Signs that need a check-up

See a doctor if bloating comes with:

  • Weight loss without trying
  • Vomiting, or difficulty swallowing
  • Blood in the stool, or black stools
  • A swollen belly that keeps growing instead of coming and going
  • New symptoms after age 45, or a family history of bowel or ovarian cancer

When it is urgent, and which tests help

Severe belly pain with a hard, swollen belly, repeated vomiting, or not passing gas or stool is an emergency. Call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Most people with gas and bloating need no tests. If symptoms continue or warning signs are present, your doctor may suggest blood tests, an ultrasound, a test for H. pylori, or an endoscopy or colonoscopy.

Foods and habits for less gas

Eat more

  • Dal, chana and rajma soaked well and cooked soft, in smaller portions
  • Curd or chaas instead of milk, if milk makes you bloated
  • Cooked vegetables such as lauki, tori, carrot and pumpkin on bad days, rather than large raw salads

Cut down

  • Sugar-free sweets, diet drinks and sugar-free gum, whose sweeteners make gas
  • Large amounts of raw onion, cabbage, cauliflower and mooli at one meal
  • A heavy meal late at night, followed straight away by bed

Good to know

  • Passing gas many times a day is normal. It is a sign of a working gut, not of disease.
  • Ajwain, hing and jeera are fine in cooking, but if bloating continues for weeks, finding the cause matters more than home remedies.

Common questions

Does gas cause chest pain?

Trapped gas in the upper belly can cause a tight or sharp pain felt in the lower chest, which usually eases after burping or passing gas. But chest pain should not be blamed on gas without care. Chest pain with sweating, breathlessness, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw is an emergency: call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Why is my stomach flat in the morning but bloated by evening?

This is typical of a sensitive gut or IBS. Food, swallowed air and gas build up through the day. It is usually harmless if it settles overnight. Bloating that stays all the time or keeps growing needs a check.

Can bloating be a sign of ovarian cancer?

Rarely. Most bloating comes from the gut. But women, especially over 50, with new bloating on most days for several weeks, feeling full quickly, or pelvic pain should see a doctor. An ultrasound and a blood test can check the ovaries.
